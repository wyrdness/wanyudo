## Overview

Wanyūdō (輪入道, 'wheel monk' or 'wheel priest') is a yōkai of Japanese folklore depicted as a flaming, single oxcart wheel with the angry, often tonsured face of a man set in its hub. It is most often connected to Kyoto and the wider Kinai region, where it is said to roll along roads at night, particularly in the vicinity of cemeteries, temples, and gates, devouring or stealing the souls of any who look upon it. A famous tale recorded in the Edo-period kaidan collection Shokoku Hyakumonogatari (1677) describes a Kyoto woman who, peeking through a sliding door at the rumbling sound outside, sees the wheel-monster carry off the legs of her child. A common protective measure recorded in folk practice is to post a strip of paper bearing the phrase 'this place is the village of Shōbo' (此所勝母の里) at the door, which is said to turn the wanyūdō away. The yokai was popularised in graphic form by Toriyama Sekien in Konjaku Gazu Zoku Hyakki (Supplement to the Hundred Demons of the Past and Present, 1779) and is closely related to katawaguruma, an earlier one-wheeled-cart yokai depicted as a woman riding a flaming wheel.

## Etymology

- **Origin**: Japanese 輪入道, composed of 輪 (wa, 'wheel') + 入道 (nyūdō, 'one who has entered the [Buddhist] path'; a tonsured monk or, more loosely, a tonsured man).
- **Meaning**: Literally 'wheel monk' or 'wheel priest'; the name combines the wheel-like body with a tonsured (often glaring) male face.
- **First use**: Documented in Edo-period kaidan literature (Shokoku Hyakumonogatari, 1677); standardised graphically by Toriyama Sekien (1779).

## Theories & Explanations

### Paranormal / Cryptozoological

**Vengeful spirit (onryō) of an executed criminal** _(Edo-period kaidan tradition)_

Some folk readings tie the wanyūdō to the spirit of a tonsured criminal punished by being broken on a wheel or dragged behind a flaming oxcart, returning as a road-haunting yokai.

### Skeptical

**Literary tradition and didactic kaidan** _(Modern yōkai studies (Komatsu Kazuhiko, Michael Dylan Foster))_

Wanyūdō is best understood as a product of Edo-period kaidan literature and yokai art rather than as an autonomous folk-belief about a real road-haunting wheel.
